TITLE: Groundnut (Peanut) Cultivation Advisory - Pest and Disease Management

IMPORTANCE
Groundnut is one of India's most important oilseed crops, widely grown in Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Maharashtra. Used for edible oil, protein-rich food, and roasted/snack consumption.

SOWING TIME
- Kharif season: second fortnight of June to first fortnight of July (with monsoon onset)
- Rabi season (as sequential crop in rice fallows, using residual moisture): last week of November to second fortnight of December
- Where irrigation is available, rabi sowing can extend to January-February, but sowing in January under residual moisture (without irrigation) risks moisture stress and poor plant stand

SOIL REQUIREMENT
Well-drained, light sandy loam or loamy soil is best suited.

LAND PREPARATION
1. Plough deeply once with a soil-turning plough
2. Follow with two cultivator ploughings to make soil fine and level
3. During final ploughing, mix Quinalphos 1.5% at 25 kg/hectare into soil to protect against termites and soil insects

FERTILIZER
- General recommendation: 20 kg Nitrogen, 50 kg Phosphorus (P2O5), 30 kg Potassium (K2O) per hectare (varies by region - confirm with local soil test)
- Gypsum application is important for better pod development
- Apply micronutrients like zinc and boron if soil test shows deficiency
- Apply well-decomposed farmyard manure or compost at 8-10 tonnes/hectare before sowing, incorporated immediately into soil
- Seed inoculation with Phosphate Solubilizing Bacteria (PSB) culture (e.g., Pseudomonas striata, Bacillus polymixa) improves phosphorus availability from soil - can be mixed with Rhizobium inoculation and applied together

IRRIGATION
- Moderate irrigation needed - avoid overwatering, which causes root rot and fungal disease
- First irrigation after sowing if rainfall is insufficient
- Critical stages needing irrigation: flowering and pod formation stage - this is when water stress most affects final yield
- Avoid waterlogging at all costs

MAJOR PESTS AND TREATMENT

1. Groundnut Leaf Miner
- Treatment: spray Profenophos 50 EC at 2 ml/litre on 30th and 45th day after sowing

2. Defoliators (leaf-eating pests, including Spodoptera)
- Treatment: Flubendiamide 48SC at 0.075 ml/litre, or Spinosad 45 SC at 2ml/litre
- Botanical/organic options: Pongamia oil at 3ml/litre, or Azadirachtin 3% at 3ml/litre, or Neem oil at 3ml/litre - shown effective in field trials
- Monitor with pheromone traps for Spodoptera: 5 traps/hectare

3. Aphids, Jassids, Thrips (sucking pests)
- Apply neem cake at 500 kg/hectare as a preventive cultural practice
- Need-based spray of Quinalphos when infestation crosses threshold

4. White Grubs (soil pest)
- Managed through spray schedule as part of community-based/area-wide approach (individual field treatment alone is less effective since grubs move between fields) - coordinate with neighboring farmers and local KVK

INTERCROPPING FOR NATURAL PEST SUPPRESSION (useful low-cost strategy)
- Pearlmillet or soybean intercrop suppresses thrips, jassids, and leaf miner
- Castor intercrop suppresses jassids and Spodoptera
- Pigeonpea intercrop suppresses early leaf spot, late leaf spot, and rust diseases
- Border cropping with pearl millet also helps as a general pest barrier

MAJOR DISEASES

1. Leaf Spot Disease (early and late leaf spot) and Rust
- Reduce incidence through crop rotation (groundnut with gram, wheat) and intercropping with pigeonpea
- Use pest/disease-resistant varieties where available (e.g., Girnar 1, ICGV 87160, ICGS 37, TAG 24 among released Indian varieties)

2. Collar Rot and Stem Rot
- Avoid deep planting of seed - shallow, correctly-depth sowing reduces infection risk
- Deep ploughing before sowing and crop rotation with gram/wheat reduces disease incidence
- Early sowing (June) and closer plant spacing (30 cm between rows) have shown lower collar rot and root rot incidence compared to wider spacing (45-60 cm)

3. Aspergillus (fungal contamination causing brown patches on cotyledons/stems)
- Caused by Aspergillus niger and A. flavus - proper drying and storage after harvest is critical to prevent this, as it's also linked to aflatoxin contamination risk in stored groundnut

HARVESTING
Ready for harvest 100-120 days after sowing, depending on variety and growing conditions.

GENERAL ADVICE
Combining resistant varieties, correct sowing depth/spacing/timing, intercropping, and need-based (not routine) pesticide use gives the most sustainable and cost-effective pest/disease control for groundnut.

WHERE TO GET HELP
Contact ICAR-Directorate of Groundnut Research (Junagadh) or your local Krishi Vigyan Kendra for region-specific variety and treatment recommendations.
